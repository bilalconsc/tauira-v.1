On the Table-Deadline-Joke Cluster: An Autoethnographic Account

March 30, 2025

This essay traces a recurring assemblage — the table-deadline-joke cluster — across three classroom encounters in Term 1 2025, reading through Barad's agential realism and Deleuze and Guattari's rhizomatics to argue that the cluster's repeated appearance reveals a structural feature of my experience as a teacher-researcher.

Research Question Alignment

RQ1
The table-deadline-joke cluster characterises my experience as a teacher-researcher through its recursive appearance across three encounters, producing each time a recognisable pattern of constraint, disruption, and affective reorientation.
RQ1a
S3, the assessment rubric, the round tables, and the spontaneous laughter each affected my being-with students in ways I could not have planned for or avoided — they were participants in the lesson's becoming.
RQ1b
The assemblages — table-deadline-joke cluster and theory-laughter bridge — name the recurring participant configurations that shaped these classroom experiences at both material and instinctual registers.

Introduction #

Three lessons. Three round tables. One recurring joke. This essay asks what the repetition means — not as a management problem or a pedagogical opportunity, but as a structural feature of the classroom encounter itself.

I draw on Karen Barad’s (2007) agential realism to argue that the assemblage I call the table-deadline-joke cluster was not produced by any single agent but emerged from the intra-action of material arrangement (the round tables), knowledge artefact (the assessment rubric), affective presence (S3’s humour), and instinctual response (laughter, boredom). I also draw on Deleuze and Guattari (1987) to read the classroom’s knowledge structure as rhizomatic — multi-entry, decentred, lateral.

Encounter One: The Deadline as Uninvited Guest (W3-L2) #

In [[enc-2025-T1-W3-L2]], the assessment rubric arrived on each desk before the students did. Laminated. Yellow. The room’s energy was flat — what I am calling boredom in the node graph. S3 named the rubric as a funeral document and the room laughed.

What Barad helps me see here is that this was not S3 acting on passive students. The joke was an intra-action: S3’s body, the rubric’s material authority, the sound of the mower outside, the round table configuration that placed certain students at the edge of the room’s attention — all of these co-produced the event. The laughter was not caused; it was assembled.

Deleuze and Guattari’s rhizome is also present here: S3’s joke entered the lesson from a lateral direction, bypassing the planned learning sequence and producing a different knowledge-entry entirely. The rubric, intended as a hierarchical guide to correct writing, was momentarily deterritorialised.

Encounter Two: The Absent Students (W4-L1) #

In [[enc-2025-T1-W4-L1]], three students were absent. Their absence structured the lesson as a trace — in Derrida’s sense, a presence-through-absence. I moved differently through the room. I spoke more carefully. The rubric-table-theory triangle assembled here: the previous week’s rubric still lying on some desks, the rearranged tables, and the introduction of Deleuzian rhizomatic thinking as the lesson’s content.

The whiteboard question — What if knowledge had no centre? — named the theoretical stakes explicitly for the first time. Students engaged cautiously. The low ambient energy of the room, the absent chairs, the question hanging in the air: these intra-acted to produce a lesson that felt unresolved even as it ended.

The Rhizome as Pedagogy #

Introducing rhizomatic thinking while the tables were arranged without a clear front produced what I want to call a performative contradiction in the best sense: the form enacted the content. Multiple entry points. No obvious centre. The students who arrived without the prior week’s context could enter anyway, from wherever they were.

Encounter Three: S3 Hands the Rhizome Back (W5-L3) #

In [[enc-2025-T1-W5-L3]], the blank whiteboard waited. S3 arrived early and rearranged a chair, then replaced it. In that gesture — the preparation and the withdrawal — I read something of what Barad means by anticipatory intra-action: S3 shaping the space before the lesson’s official beginning.

When S3 rephrased the rhizome as “everything connects to everything and nothing connects to anything”, the room laughed. But this was different laughter from W3. It was not the laughter of relief from the rubric’s authority. It was recognition — the sound of a room finding itself in a concept it had been living with for two weeks.

The theory-laughter bridge assembled here: node-004 (Rhizomatic Thinking) and node-006 (Laughter) in their closest conjunction. See [[memo-003]] for the accepted gap suggestion that the blank whiteboard deserves its own node.

Conclusion #

The table-deadline-joke cluster is not a metaphor. It is a naming of an assemblage that structured three lessons, produced affective shifts I could not predict, and put pressure on my understanding of what teaching is. Barad asks us to take material participants seriously as co-producers of knowledge events. Deleuze and Guattari ask us to abandon the root-tree model of knowledge in favour of the rhizome. Both framings illuminate something the cluster makes visible: that my experience as a teacher-researcher is constituted with participants, not for them.
