Narrating Growth Diffractively

March 30, 2025

This essay traces three classroom encounters across Term 1 2025 through Tauira's rhizomatic analysis, arguing that prescribed reflective practice does not simply document teaching but scripts how personal growth should be narrated. Reading the encounters through Barad's agential realism, Deleuze and Guattari's rhizomatics, and more conventional reflective models such as Kolb, Schön, and Gibbs, I suggest that Tauira makes visible a distributed field of affect, materiality, legitimacy, and pedagogical instability that exceeds the usual self-improving reflective account.

Research Question Alignment

RQ1
The three encounters characterise my experience as a teacher-researcher by showing how prescribed reflection repeatedly narrows pedagogical events into legible narratives of success, change, and growth.
RQ1a
The worksheet, desks, writing task, video medium, schema concept, sadness, and my own shifting roles affected my being-with students in ways I could not fully plan for or stabilise.
RQ1b
The assemblages — worksheet-compliance cluster, writing-video-rigour-recognition cluster, and concept-affect-threshold cluster — name recurring participant configurations that shaped these classroom experiences across material, affective, and discursive registers.

Introduction #

Reflection in professional practice, especially in teacher education, is at a tipping point. In contemporary teacher education, reflection often takes the form of evidence in prescribed post-lesson commentary (ref). Such routines may support accountability and professional discipline, but they could also narrow what counts as learning (ref). Over time, the routines become a script for how both professional and personal growth should be narrated. In this essay, I examine scripting as the central aspect of reflection in my own professional practice. This inquiry matters to me personally and professionally because it extends my progression as an educator-researcher from the module EDU7393 (appendix) and the wider PGDEI programme. The EDU 7393 inquiry began with discomfort toward the convenience of prescribed reflective routines. That discomfort pushed me to ask not whether reflection matters, but what kind of reflection becomes possible when classroom life is approached as relational, affective, and materially entangled. In this essay, I move beyond treating classroom encounters as a scene for retrospective self-improvement. Drawing on evidence and its analysis curated through Tauira (website), I argue that prescribed reflective practices do not merely document experience and that they regulate how professional growth could be narrated.

Defining the Aspect #

The aspect I examine here is the way prescribed reflection scripts professional and personal growth into a familiar story of criticism, adaptability, and improvement. Reflective templates not only invite teachers to think but also organise how that thinking should appear (ref). The teacher is assumed to be a reflective, critical, progressive, ethical, and improving professional (ref), while the complexity of pedagogical life itself is reduced to docile afterthoughts. To understand the importance of the aspect, I focus on the literature on diffraction and reflective practice. Bozalek and Zembylas describe diffraction as an “otherwise” methodology that attends to how bodies, materials, and discourses intra-act, rather than presuming a detached subject who reflects on the world from a distance. Hill’s “diffractive practitioner” similarly challenges reflective models that overlook affect, matter, place, and more-than-human agencies in learning. The authors bring our attention to the prescribed reflection, which centralises the reflective subject and underreads the relations that construct classroom life. Tauira is crucial here because it supports this essay and a different kind of inquiry. In my research project, Tauira is a website-based knowledge environment where encounters, fieldnotes, memos, artefacts, and interaction maps are gathered and revisited across practice. It is not simply a repository. It functions as a relation-disclosing object that allows pedagogical moments to be reread across bodies, objects, timings, affects, and media. Thus, Tauira is not only evidence of my individual competence or growth.

Context of Practice #

The essay draws on encounters from my professional teaching practice, especially within IBDP Psychology lessons and portfolio-based reflection in the PGDEI programme. This context matters because it combines deep conceptual understanding with institutional expectations for evidence, progress, and reflective accountability. In such settings, there is strong pressure to demonstrate rigour, show impact, and clearly narrate improvement. In the current context, there are also distinctive opportunities and constraints. The curriculum invites conceptual depth, critical thought, and ethical questioning. Moreover, classroom artefacts, assessment routines, time pressures, student identities, and institutional formats are all coparticipants in what the lesson becomes. Ethics is embedded throughout this essay on anonymity, power relations, inclusivity, and the consequences of pedagogical action. For that reason, the evidence used here is anonymised and treated not as raw data, but as part of my professional inquiry.

Reflective Investigation #

a. Identifying the Issue #

The evidence for this essay consists primarily of post-lesson reflections and critical incident reports gathered in the professional development portfolio and curated through Tauira. These materials are useful because they make visible the structure of prescribed reflection. The template does not simply ask what happened in a lesson; it already organises response into what was successful, what should change, and what this means for personal growth. The issue, then, is not only what I say in reflection, but how the form of reflection pre-narrates what legitimate growth should look like.

b. Intervention and Inquiry #

The task at hand was to reread three encounters, firstly through the reflective models such as Gibbs, Kolb, and Schön, and secondly, one encounter diffractively through Tauira. In the first encounter, a lesson on affordance appeared strong during the planning phase. Nonetheless, the classroom lost energy when students shifted from conceptual understanding to applying creative design. When read with Gibbs or Schön, this encounter could be narrated as a pacing issue and therefore as an opportunity for better future adjustment (ref). However, when read diffractively, the same encounter was perceived differently. The students’ worksheet directed attention toward correctness. The lesson sequence favoured analytic performance. The desks arranged in traditional rows oriented students toward paper and screen. My own desire for visible evidence intensified micro-management. The issue was not with my technique for facilitation. Rather, an assemblage had produced a mode of participation (ref) in which students could comply without entering a thorough relation with the task or one another. The second encounter involved a written homework task. I learned that a student regularly created informational content on YouTube. My immediate reflective note framed this as differentiation and student-centred planning. Again, conventional reflective models could translate the moment into a positive story of responsiveness and professional growth (ref). As expected, diffractive rereading unsettled this neat account of the encounter. The encounter exposed how strongly I default to written prose as the fitting medium of seriousness, rigour, and assessable understanding. What changed was not merely the homework format for the student, but also the arrangement of medium, legitimacy, assessment, and my own pedagogical comfort. The third encounter took place during a lesson on schema, stereotype, and culture. A student recognised that his personal view had been shaped by social and cultural conditioning. This led to confusion and sadness. A conventional reflective cycle could help me look at this encounter as one requiring sensitivity, care, and improved facilitation. Conversely, a diffractive reading resisted reducing it to a better future technique. The sadness in the room emerged through concept, self-awareness, identity, memory, classroom tempo, institutional expectations, and my own unstable roles as teacher, witness, caretaker, and researcher. What appeared, when read diffractively, was a pedagogical threshold in which learning happened through discomfort, affect, and relational exposure. Investigating the encounter through Tauira disclosed relations among not only the co-participants in the individual encounters but also across the three encounters (see Fig. 2).

In this encounter the co-particpants are categorised into material (M), knowledge (K), and instincts (I) (link). The co-participants are represented by the nodes (green, yellow, and purple dots) as in Tauira (see figure 3). Specifically, in this encounter, the nodes include Schema and Stereotype Concept (K), Student Identity (K), Analytic Worksheet (M), Board Language (K), Sadness (I), Classroom Tempo (I), Role Instability (I).

Figure 3. Details of Tauira Rhizomatic Map. Let us examine the cluster three by reading it rhizomatically through four lenses. Firstly, the rhizome does not have a centre, but it has zones where more connections pass through than anywhere else. In Cluster 3, Sadness is that intensity. It pulls the two highest-weight incoming edges (see Figure 4) in the entire graph; an affect relation from Schema/Stereotype Concept (weight 0.90); and a mediation from Student Identity (weight 0.85). It then disrupts Role Instability (0.80). No other node across all three encounters/clusters carries this combination of incoming weight and outward disruption. Sadness is the node through which the most pedagogical force is passing. In rhizomatic terms, it is a plateau. A region of sustained intensity that organises the entire cluster around itself without claiming to be an origin or a conclusion.

Secondly, Schema and Stereotype Concept is the structural bridge of this cluster. On one side it connects to Board Language (K), my deliberate conceptual framing in words on the board, via co-presence (0.60). On the other side, Schema/Stereotype Concept carries an affect edge of weight 0.90 directly into Sadness. It is the single node through which the lesson as designed crosses into the lesson as felt. Without this bridge, Board Language stays a pedagogical object and the concept stays academic. It is Schema/Stereotype Concept that transforms curriculum into encounter. In diffractive terms, this is where the wave passes through the slit and the interference pattern begins. Thirdly, Cluster 3 forms two parallel tracks that never intersect with an edge (See Figure 3). The first track, Track A, (Conceptual→Affective) moves from Board Language → Schema/Stereotype Concept → Sadness → Role Instability. Four nodes, three directed edges, average weight 0.77. This track moves from institutional inscription through conceptual recognition, through biographical mediation (Student Identity enters here at 0.85), into Sadness, and then outward as disruption of role. It escalates with each step. Whereas, the second track, Track B (Material → Temporal) moves from Analytic Worksheet to Classroom Tempo. Two nodes, one directed edge, weight 0.65. This track runs independently and quietly alongside Track A. The worksheet keeps the lesson structurally moving and Classroom Tempo is its direct product. The most significant structural feature of this cluster is the absence of any edge between Track A and Track B. The room’s institutional machinery (Track B) and the room’s affective life (Track A) are fully co-present but not relationally connected in the graph. This is the lesson’s central problem made visible. Fourthly, Role Instability is the most peripheral node in the cluster that is one incoming edge and no outgoing edges within the cluster. It is a terminal node in the graph. Structurally this means the teacher-researcher’s instability as a role is recorded as an event but has no mapped consequences yet. In rhizomatic terms, Role Instability is an open line of flight that is the point where the assemblage exceeds its own current mapping. It marks where the graph has not yet grown. Board Language is similarly peripheral on the other end. Furthermore, conducting pattern analysis on Cluster 3 five revealed five patterns including recurrence, affinity, transversal links, bifurcation, and convergence. The tag ‘threshold’ appears on three of the seven Cluster 3 nodes: Schema/Stereotype Concept, Sadness, and Classroom Tempo. This is the highest threshold density of any encounter in the graph. The cluster maps a sustained threshold condition where the lesson keeps moving into territory it cannot step back from. Recurrence of legibility across Board Language and Schema/Stereotype Concept also shows that the institutional demand to name and make-visible the concepts and understanding persists even as the affective terrain shifts. The two nodes with the strongest affinity to Sadness approach it from entirely different directions. One from curriculum (Schema/Stereotype Concept, affect, 0.90) and one from biography (Student Identity, mediates, 0.85). This convergence is not a coincidence of the lesson plan instead it is a structural inevitability. Any lesson that asks students to recognise schemas and stereotypes conceptually, while those students carry personal cultural histories, will produce Sadness (ref). The graph makes this structural causality visible. Student Identity is the most transversal node as it mediates across the biographical and the conceptual and feeding directly into Sadness. Nonetheless, it shares no edge with any node in Track B. The student’s personal history enters the conceptual chain but is not affected by the material or institutional infrastructure. The Analytic Worksheet does not ask about identity. Classroom Tempo does not slow Analytic Worksheet. Board Language does not name the Analytic Worksheet. Student Identity operates transversally as it cuts across the conceptual underpinnings without being acknowledged by the material. This could be the cluster’s central ethical gap. The bifurcation in Cluster 3 is not between two subjects (as in Cluster 2’s teacher-discomfort vs. student-enthusiasm split) but between institutional time and affective depth. Track A deepens with each edge and Track B continues at uniform weight. They bifurcate from the same moment as the lesson commence and run in parallel without reuniting. All meaningful force converges on Sadness (combined incoming weight 1.75, the highest in the graph) and then channels through a single disrupting edge into Role Instability. The convergence-then-singular-exit pattern is the structural signature of a threshold event (ref). Role Instability as the sole exit node means the encounter’s primary consequence is the teacher-researcher’s own instability. But is it the case when we read these emerging relations diffractively? Diffraction, as Karen Barad theorises it, is not the reflection of one thing in another rather it is what happens when waves pass through each other and produce an interference pattern that was not present in either wave alone (ref). A diffractive reading does not ask what did the teacher see in the student or what did the lesson produce. Instead it asks what pattern emerges at the point of interference between the lesson-as-designed and the lesson-as-lived? In Cluster 3, the lesson-as-designed enters the room through two material-epistemic nodes (Board Language and Analytic Worksheet). Board Language inscribes the concepts of schema, stereotype, and culture on the board which keeps the lesson pedagogically legible by holding its conceptual framework steady in the room. Analytic Worksheet keeps bodies oriented toward task completion and thus producing Classroom Tempo. These two nodes constitute what Barad would call the apparatus constituted by the material-discursive arrangement through which the phenomenon of “lesson” is produced and measured. The diffractivestory does not end here as enters this apparatus another wave entirely. A wave that the apparatus was not designed to receive. Schema/Stereotype Concept, the very content the apparatus is delivering, carries within it a recognition function that exceeds its curricular framework. Students encounter the concept of stereotype not as an abstract category but as a description of what they have personally lived and experienced without being aware. It moves with a weight of 0.90 into Sadness. Simultaneously, Student Identity enters from, the biographical histories students carry in their bodies such as the cultural memories, the embodied experiences of being stereotyped, the costs of recognition, an entirely different direction. Student Identity mediates this crossing at 0.85. Here is where the interference pattern emerges. Two waves (one curricular: Schema/Stereotype Concept, arriving from Board Language and one biographical: Student Identity, arriving from outside the apparatus entirely) pass through Sadness. Neither wave alone would have produced what emerges. The curriculum without Student Identity would have remained an academic exercise. Student Identity without the curriculum would have stayed private, unaddressed in the classroom space. It is only at this intersection where the concept and the biography meet that Sadness becomes possible, necessary, and real. I do not consider this encounter as a teaching failure. This is not an unintended side effect. In Barad’s framework, this is the only phenomenon, atleast for the current analysis, that was happening in the room (ref). The lesson did not accidentally produce Sadness as the lesson was always already an encounter between conceptual frames and lived bodies (ref). Sadness is the pattern that interference makes visible. What the diffractive reading then demands attention to is what happens downstream of this interference. Sadness does not dissolve into the lesson. It does not become an assessment output. It does not enter the Analytic Worksheet. It cannot because there is no edge from Sadness to Track B. Instead, Sadness fires a single outgoing disrupting edge (0.80) into Role Instability. The teacher-researcher, who stood in the room as a teacher, a witness, a caretaker, and a researcher simultaneously, finds all four of those roles made incommensurable in a single moment. The teaching role says, “continue the lesson”. The witness role says, “stop and acknowledge”. The caretaker role says, “handle this carefully”. The researcher role says, “record what you are seeing”. None of these can be fully enacted at the same time. Role Instability is not confusion rather it is the correct response to an impossible co-presence of ethical and professional practioner demands. Moreover, the diffractive reading further illuminates what the graph does not yet contain. Track B, Analytic Worksheet enabling Classroom Tempo, carries no disruption, no affect, no acknowledgement of what Track A just produced. This is not because the teacher was inattentive rather it is because the apparatus itself has no receiver for this frequency. The worksheet was not designed to ask, “what did it cost you to recognise this?”. Classroom Tempo was not designed to slow when sadness enters. The diffractive reading makes this understading of structural insensitivity not as a personal failure of pedagogy but as an intra-active limitation of the apparatus. It reveals a diffractive gap between what curricula ask students to know and what it costs emotionally to know it, a gap that no worksheet, no board, no institutional clock is currently equipped to hold. Role Instability is where I stepped into that gap. The graph maps it.

c. Professional Learning #

Across these encounters and specifically the diffractive reading, my professional learning has shifted from seeing reflection as retrospective self-correction toward understanding it as rereading entangled pedagogical events. Kolb, Gibbs, and Schön remain useful because they help organise experience and return the practitioner to practice with discipline. But they also tend to position the teacher as the central subject of the reflective account. In contrast, diffractive rereading made it harder to narrate growth as a tidy sequence of noticing, adjusting, and improving. What I learned about teaching, through the diffractive way of reflecting on the encounters, is that pedagogy is never only cognitive, never only human, and never fully contained by reflective templates. What I learned about myself is that my own professional habits and practice are shaped by institutional desires for evidence, clarity, legitimacy, and control. Growth, then, is the disruption of the very narrative through which I had learned to recognise myself as a “good” reflective teacher.

Implications #

The implications of this inquiry for me as a teacher-researcher are fourfold. As a learner, it suggests that meaningful learning is not exhausted by visible performance or conceptually correct responses and affect, recognition, uncertainty, and medium also matter. For my practice, it means I need to design with greater attention to relation, materiality, and the affective consequences of pedagogical decisions rather than relying only on retrospective adjustment. For my context, the inquiry suggests that reflective systems should not only ask teachers what worked and what they would change, but also what relations, assumptions, and institutional pressures shaped the classroom encounter in the first place. This matters for leadership as well as reflection. Fullan (ref) argues that effective leadership generates internal commitment rather than external compliance, and that externally defined goals and steps tend to produce only short-term, less durable engagement. In that sense, prescribed reflective routines risk securing evidence of professionalism without necessarily triggering deeper professional investment in research inquiry. If reflection is to support meaningful growth, it must do more than organise compliance with institutional expectations. It must help generate internally committed engagement with the complex conditions of teaching and learning life. For the future, Tauira offers a way of extending that work by connecting encounters, artefacts, memos, and maps so that reflection becomes an ongoing real-time professional inquiry rather than a mere collection of archived evidence. Seen through Fullan’s account of change (ref), this also develops me not only as a reflective practitioner but as a leader of learning concerned with ethics, relationships, contextual learning, and the conditions through which meaningful change becomes possible.

Conclusion #

This essay has argued with evidence that prescribed reflective practices support professional learning by scripting how personal growth should be narrated. By rereading portfolio evidence through Tauira and through a diffractive framework, I have tried to show that pedagogical experience exceeds that containment. It emerges through relations among bodies, concepts, objects, affects, timings, media, and institutional forms. To reflect and to narrate growth otherwise is not to abandon reflection. It is to resist reducing professional growth to a neat story told after the lesson. Read through Fullan, the point is not simply to reflect more efficiently, but to cultivate ways of professional learning, such as Tauira, that generate internal commitment rather than external compliance. From that perspective, the work of reflection becomes less about proving that I have improved and more about learning how teaching and the teacher are continually being made within entangled pedagogical life.
