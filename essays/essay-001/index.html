Narrating Growth Diffractively

March 30, 2025

This essay traces three classroom encounters across Term 1 2025 through Tauira's rhizomatic analysis, arguing that prescribed reflective practice does not simply document teaching but scripts how personal growth should be narrated. Reading the encounters through Barad's agential realism, Deleuze and Guattari's rhizomatics, and more conventional reflective models such as Kolb, Schön, and Gibbs, I suggest that Tauira makes visible a distributed field of affect, materiality, legitimacy, and pedagogical instability that exceeds the usual self-improving reflective account.

Research Question Alignment

RQ1
The three encounters characterise my experience as a teacher-researcher by showing how prescribed reflection repeatedly narrows pedagogical events into legible narratives of success, change, and growth.
RQ1a
The worksheet, desks, writing task, video medium, schema concept, sadness, and my own shifting roles affected my being-with students in ways I could not fully plan for or stabilise.
RQ1b
The assemblages — worksheet-compliance cluster, writing-video-rigour-recognition cluster, and concept-affect-threshold cluster — name recurring participant configurations that shaped these classroom experiences across material, affective, and discursive registers.

Introduction #

Reflection in professional practice, especially in educator education, is at a tipping point. In contemporary educator education, reflection often takes the form of evidence in prescribed post-lesson commentary. Such routines may support accountability and professional discipline, but they could also narrow what counts as learning (Schön, 1983; Gibbs, 1988; Bozalek and Zembylas, 2017). Over time, the routines become a script for how both professional and personal growth should be narrated. In this essay, I examine narrative scripting as the central aspect of reflection in my own professional practice. This inquiry matters to me personally and professionally because it advances my progression as an educator-researcher, building on the modules and the wider PGDEI programme. The inquiry began with discomfort toward the convenience of prescribed reflective routines. This discomfort pushed me to ask not whether reflection matters, but what kind of reflection becomes possible when classroom life is approached as relational, affective, and materially entangled (Barad, 2007; Bozalek and Zembylas, 2017; Hill, 2017; Lambert, 2021). In this essay, I move beyond treating classroom encounters as a backdrop for retrospective self-improvement. Drawing on evidence and its analysis curated through Tauira, I argue that prescribed reflective practices should not exclusively document experience and that they regulate how professional growth could be narrated diffractively.

Defining the Aspect #

The aspect I examine here is the way prescribed reflection scripts professional and personal growth into a familiar story arc of criticism, adaptability, and improvement. Reflective templates not only invite educators to think but also organise how that thinking should appear. The educator is assumed to be a reflective, critical, progressive, ethical, and improving professional (Kolb, 1984; Gibbs, 1988; Schön, 1983), while the complexity of pedagogical life itself is reduced to docile afterthoughts on the performance of the educator. Diffractive literature offers a pragmatic challenge to this logic. Barad (2007) argues that diffraction is not a mirror of sameness but is awareness of differing patterns produced through the entanglement of participants. Diffraction, a word adopted from physics by Karen Barad, is not the reflection of one thing in another. It is what happens when waves interact and produce an interference pattern that was not present in either wave alone (Barad, 2007). Then, a diffractive reading of the classroom encounters asks what pattern emerges at the point of interference between the lesson-as-designed and the lesson-as-lived? Developing on this, Bozalek and Zembylas (2017) contrast reflection with diffraction, arguing that reflection often assumes a bounded subject who looks back on experience, whereas diffraction attends to how bodies, discourses, materialities, and histories intra-act in the emergence of the classroom encounters. Hill (2017) extends this, in the education context, through the role of the diffractive practitioner. Meanwhile, Lambert (2021) and Nociti (2024) show how diffractive practice can edify educator learning in ways that exceed retrospective self-appraisal. Such a framework aligns with complex accounts of educator learning in which practice is not linear and individual but emergent, distributed, and shaped by multiple forces (Strom and Viesca, 2023). Tauira is imperative here because it supports this essay and a diffractive inquiry. In my research project, Tauira is a website-based knowledge environment where encounters, fieldnotes, memos, artefacts, homework, essays, and interaction maps are gathered and revisited across practice over time. It is not simply a repository. It functions as a relation-disclosing object that allows pedagogical moments to be reread across bodies, objects, timings, space, affects, and media. Therefore, Tauira is not only evidence of my individual competence or growth.

Context of Practice #

The essay draws on encounters from my professional teaching practice, especially within IB MYP/DP Psychology lessons and portfolio-based reflection in the PGDEI programme. This context matters because it merges deep conceptual understanding with institutional expectations for evidence, progress, and reflective accountability. In such professional settings, there is strong pressure to demonstrate rigour, show impact, and clearly narrate improvement. The classroom, in the current context, is a situated arrangement of learners (IB MYP/DP), concepts (schema/stereotype), worksheets, boards, desks, expectations, and assessments. Situated learning theory helps explain why meaning cannot be detached from the terms of participation in which it emerges (Clancey, 1995). The classroom encounters, discussed here, involve vulnerability, anonymity, and interpretation. Allowing for relational traces of pedagogical life that demand care in how they are represented and reread (Noddings, 1984; Palmer, 1998; Ergas, 2018).

Reflective Investigation #

a. Identifying the Issue #

The evidence for this essay consists primarily of post-lesson reflections and critical incident reports gathered in the professional development portfolio and curated through Tauira. These materials are useful because they make visible the structure of prescribed reflection. The template does not simply ask what happened in a lesson; it already organises response into what was successful, what should change, and what this means for personal growth. The issue, then, is not only what I say in reflection, but how the form of reflection pre-narrates what legitimate growth should look like.

b. Intervention and Inquiry #

The task at hand was to review three classroom encounters. First, each encounter was examined through the classic reflective models such as Gibbs, Kolb, and Schön. The purpose was not to reject reflective models altogether, but to assess what they make effortless to say and what they make challenging to notice. Second, one encounter was reread diffractively through Tauira. In the first encounter, a lesson on affordance appeared strong during the planning phase. Nonetheless, the classroom lost energy when students shifted from conceptual understanding to applying creative design. When read with Gibbs or Schön, this encounter could be narrated as a pacing issue and therefore as an opportunity for better future adjustment (Gibbs, 1988; Schön, 1983). However, when read diffractively, the same encounter was perceived differently. The students’ worksheet directed attention toward correctness. The lesson sequence favoured analytic performance. The desks arranged in traditional rows oriented students toward paper and screen. My own desire for visible evidence of learning intensified micro-management. The problem was not with my technique for facilitating learning experiences. Instead, an assemblage had created a mode of participation in which students could satisfy without entering a thorough relation with the task or one another. The second encounter involved a written homework task. I learned that a student regularly created informational content on YouTube. My immediate reflective note recognised this as differentiation and student-centred planning. Again, conventional reflective models could translate the moment into a positive story of responsiveness and professional growth (Gibbs, 1988; Schön, 1983). As expected, the diffractive account of the encounter unsettled this neat translation. The encounter exposed how strongly I default to written prose as the apt medium of seriousness, rigour, and assessable understanding. The pedagogical shift, therefore, was not simply from one homework format to another. It involved a reconfiguration of validity, medium, assessment, and my own pedagogical comfort. Constructivist and situated accounts of learning help explain that knowledge is not neutral with respect to the forms in which it is produced and demonstrated, and disciplinary participation can be enabled or constrained by the media that institutions value (Clancey, 1995; Bada and Olusegun, 2015; Peden and VanVoorhis, 2023). The third encounter took place during a lesson on schema, stereotype, and culture. A student recognised that her personal view had been shaped by social and cultural conditioning. This led to confusion and sadness, an emotional turmoil. A conventional reflective cycle could help me look at this encounter as one requiring sensitivity, care, and improved facilitation of learning experiences. Conversely, a diffractive reading resisted reducing it to a better future technique. The sadness in the room emerged through concept, self-awareness, identity, memory, classroom tempo, institutional expectations, and my own unstable roles as educator, witness, caretaker, and researcher. What appeared, when read diffractively, was a pedagogical threshold in which learning happened through discomfort, affect, and relational exposure rather than through cognitive mastery alone (Meyer and Land, 2003; Meyer and Land, 2006; Noddings, 1984). At this point, Tauira made a difference because it allowed the encounter to be mapped relationally rather than summarised retrospectively. Figure 1 brings together the three encounters and the possible relations mapped around sadness within encounter (cluster) three. The figure is useful because it makes visible the density of connections through which the encounter took shape.

Within Cluster 3, the mapped co-participants were categorised as material (M), knowledge (K), and instincts (I). The co-participants are represented by the nodes (green, yellow, and purple dots) as in Tauira (see figure 2). Figure 3 made the hidden four attributes of the third encounter visible. First, the concentration of force around sadness in the cluster is because several relations converged around it. Second, the Schema and Stereotype Concept operated as the link between the lesson-as-designed and the lesson-as-lived. The concept traversed into the student’s affective life through recognition that surpassed the planned academic task. Third, the cluster displayed two parallel tracks. One track connected board language, conceptual recognition, sadness, and role instability. The other track connected worksheet structure and classroom tempo. Oddly, these tracks were coexisting without being sufficiently integrated. This means the lesson kept moving, while the affective event unfolded on a different spatiotemporal plane. Fourth, Role Instability appeared as an unresolved line of flight, suggesting that the educator-researcher’s ethical and professional positioning had been disrupted without being fully transformed.

The diffractive story does not end here, as another wave entirely enters this apparatus. A wave that the apparatus was not designed to receive. Students encounter the concept of stereotype not as an abstract category but as a description of what they have personally lived and experienced without being aware of it. Simultaneously, student identity enters from the biographical histories students carry in their bodies, such as the cultural memories, the embodied experiences of being stereotyped, the costs of recognition, and an entirely different direction. Both the concept and student identity converge into sadness. Here is where the interference pattern emerges. Two waves (one curricular, Schema/Stereotype Concept, arriving from Board Language and one biographical, student identity, arriving from outside the apparatus entirely) pass through Sadness. Neither wave alone would have produced what emerges. The curriculum without student identity would have remained an academic exercise. Student identity without the curriculum would have stayed private, unaddressed in the classroom space. It is only at this intersection where the material, knowledge, and instinct meet that Sadness becomes possible, necessary, and real. I do not consider this encounter a teaching failure. This is not an unintended side effect. In Barad’s framework, this is the only phenomenon, at least for the current analysis, that was happening in the room (Barad, 2007). The lesson did not accidentally produce sadness, as the lesson was always already an encounter between conceptual frames and lived bodies. Sadness is the pattern that interference makes visible. What the diffractive reading then demands attention to is what happens downstream of this interference, and its implications for me. Sadness does not dissolve into the lesson. It does not become an assessment output. It does not enter the Analytic Worksheet. It cannot because there is no edge from Sadness to Track B (see Figure 3). Instead, Sadness fires a single outgoing disruption into role instability. The educator-researcher, who stood in the room as an educator, a witness, a caretaker, and a researcher simultaneously, finds all four of those roles made incommensurable in a single moment. The teaching role says, “continue the lesson”. The witness role says, “stop and acknowledge”. The caretaker role says, “handle this carefully”. The researcher’s role says, “record what you are seeing”. None of these can be fully enacted at the same time. Role instability, then, is the correct response to an impossible co-presence of ethical and professional practitioner demands. These findings are supported conceptually by diffractive methodology, ecological accounts of relational environment, and work on interaction-dominant systems. Diffractive methodology helps explain why the event should not be reduced to an educator observing a student from outside the phenomenon (Barad, 2007; Bozalek and Zembylas, 2017). Ecological and affordance-based literature helps explain why classroom arrangements, artefacts, and action possibilities matter as co-creators of what could happen (Gibson, 2014; Maier and Fadel, 2009; Read and Szokolszky, 2020). Work on complex and interaction-dominant systems further supports a non-linear perspective in which the lesson cannot be understood as a simple chain of causes located within individual minds alone (Wallot and Kelty-Stephen, 2018; Strom and Viesca, 2023).

c. Professional Learning #

Across these encounters and specifically the diffractive reading, my professional learning has shifted from seeing reflection as retrospective self-correction toward understanding it as rereading entangled pedagogical events. Kolb, Gibbs, and Schön remain useful because they help organise experience and return the practitioner to practice with discipline. But they also tend to position the educator as the central focus of the reflective account. In contrast, diffraction made it harder to narrate growth as a tidy sequence of noticing, adjusting, and improving. What I learned about teaching, through the diffractive way of reflecting on the encounters, is that pedagogy is never only cognitive, never only human, and never fully contained by reflective templates. What I learned about myself is that my own professional habits and practice are shaped by institutional desires for evidence, clarity, validity, and control. Growth, then, is the disruption of the very narrative through which I had learned to recognise myself as a “good” reflective educator.

Implications #

The implications of this inquiry for me as an educator-researcher are fourfold. First, for learning, it suggests that meaningful learning is not exhausted by visible performance or conceptually correct responses. Affect, recognition, uncertainty, and medium also matter. This is consistent with constructivist, situated, and threshold accounts of learning, all of which resist reducing knowledge to discrete outcomes detached from the terms of participation (Clancey, 1995; Bada and Olusegun, 2015; Meyer and Land, 2006). For my practice, it means I need to design learning experiences with greater attention to relation, materiality, and the affective consequences of pedagogical decisions rather than relying only on retrospective adjustment. Second, for pedagogy, it suggests that design must attend more carefully to materiality and affect. Classroom tasks, room arrangements, representational modes, and time structures are not neutral to teaching apparatus. Third, for reflective systems, it suggests that educators should not only ask what worked and what they would change, but also what relations, assumptions, and institutional pressures shaped the classroom encounter in the first place. This matters for leadership as well as reflection. Fullan (2002) argues that effective leadership generates intrinsic motivation rather than external compliance, and that externally defined goals and actions tend to produce only short-term, less durable engagement. In that sense, prescribed reflective routines are uncertain in securing evidence of professionalism without necessarily triggering deeper professional commitment in research inquiry. If reflection is to support meaningful growth, it must do more than organise compliance with institutional expectations. It must help generate internally committed engagement with the complex conditions of teaching and learning life. Fourth, for future practice, Tauira offers a way of extending reflection into ongoing professional inquiry. By connecting encounters, artefacts, memos, and maps, reflection becomes an ongoing, real-time professional inquiry rather than a mere collection of archived evidence. This aligns with evidence-informed practice in a broader sense, but it also resists reducing evidence to what can be easily standardised or counted.

Conclusion #

This essay has argued with evidence that prescribed reflective practices support professional learning by scripting how personal growth should be narrated. By rereading portfolio evidence through Tauira and through a diffractive framework, I have tried to show that pedagogical experience exceeds that containment. It emerges through relations among bodies, concepts, objects, affects, timings, media, and institutional forms. To reflect and to narrate growth otherwise is not to abandon reflection. It is to resist reducing professional growth to a neat story told after the lesson. Read through Fullan, the point is to cultivate ways of professional learning, such as Tauira. From this perspective, the work of reflection becomes less about proving that I have improved and more about learning how teaching and the educator are continually being made within entangled pedagogical life.
