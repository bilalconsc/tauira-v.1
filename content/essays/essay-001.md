## Introduction

Reflection in professional practices, such as teacher education, is at a tipping point. In contemporary teacher education reflection takes the form of evidence in prescribed post-lesson commentary. The questions in Fig. 1, present with no doubt a case for the usefulness of such evidence, provided the quality and quantity of questions. However, such a routine could compromise the quality of professional learning (ref). Over time, the routine regresses from being a tool for productivity to scripting how personal growth should be narrated. In this essay, I examine scripting as the central aspect of reflection in contemporary professional contexts. I ask how prescribed post-lesson reflective practice shapes the teacher as a self-observing, self-improving subject, and what becomes possible when that script is reread diffractively rather than simply repeated. (ref) This inquiry matters to me personally and professionally because it extends and evidence my progression as an educator-researcher from EDU7393 and from the wider PGDEI programme. My earlier inquiry in EDU 7393 began with my discomfort with the convenience of a prescribed reflective routine. The discomfort pushed me to ask not whether reflection matters, rather it pushed me to ask what kind of reflection becomes possible when classroom life is approached as relational, affective, and materially entangled rather than as a scene for retrospective self-improvement. (ref) I argue that prescribed reflective practices do not merely document experience and that they regulate how professional growth should be narrated. Through Tauira and diffractive rereading, I explore how pedagogical learning emerges otherwise. (ref).

## Tauira as a Diffractive Object

To pursue this inquiry, I use Tauira as part of the method and part of the object of study. Tauira is intended to be an intelligent website-based knowledge environment in which encounters, fieldnotes, essays, memos, artefacts, and rhizomatic maps are gathered and revisited across the professional teaching practice. It matters in the present context because this essay is concerned with whether reflective practice can move beyond receding to the reflective “I.” and Tauira offers a way of tracing pedagogical experience without immediately collapsing it back into a singular teacher narrative. This is also why Tauira can be approached as a diffractive object. (ref)
Tauira participates in how my experience is revisited, related, patterned, and made available for further probe. Thus, Tauira is relation-disclosing. It allows classroom moments to be reread across bodies, objects, expectations, timings, media, and affects, rather than being captured only as evidence of my individual competence or growth. (ref)

## Diffractive Reflection as Framework

Diffraction is the most promising candidate for the conceptual orientation of this inquiry. In educational and teaching practice research, diffraction has been proposed as an “otherwise” methodology that attends to how bodies, materials, affects, and discourses intra-act, rather than assuming a detached subject who reflects upon their world of experience from a distance. (ref) Whereas, conventional reflective models often retain the image of a teacher who stands outside experience, looks back upon it, and narrates improvement from an institutionally secure interpretive position. (ref) In contrast, a diffractive approach analyses insights through one another, attends to interference and entanglement, and treats knowledge as emerging from relations rather than from an isolated reflective consciousness. (ref)
In the present inquiry, the diffractive framework constitutes sociomaterial thinking, assemblage, and being-with. Sociomaterial perspectives resist the idea that pedagogy is only cognitive or only human. They try to show how teaching is transformed by conversations, technologies, prompts, institutional discourses, classroom artefacts, and embodied responses. (ref) 
Assemblage, as I use it here, refers to the temporary gathering of bodies, emotions, objects, expectations, and knowledge through which classroom encounters are produced. (ref) Being-with clarifies that my experiences as an educator-researcher, understanding, practice, and reflection are co-constituted in relation to students, tasks, materials, and circumstances rather than originating in an autonomous self. (ref) A bit on encounter as well…These concepts matter because they allow me to reread professional reflection as an evidence of what I learned. They further support me to map the relations of the participants engaged in a classroom encounter as interconnected, non-linear networks rather than hierarchies. I (ref)

### Evidence: How Growth Is Scripted

The evidence for this essay consists primarily of post-lesson reflections and critical incident records gathered in the professional development portfolio. These materials are especially useful because they make visible the structure of prescribed reflection. The template does not simply ask what happened in a lesson. It already organises response into success, future change, and personal growth (See Figure 1). In doing so, it shapes the teacher into appearing reflective, critical, progressive, ethical, and improving (ref). 
My diffractive rereading of this evidence is therefore an attempt to notice what such a reflective format omits or flattens. The reflective template can capture useful afterthoughts, but it can also compress classroom encounters into acceptable professional scripts. The template (See Figure 1) encourages me to identify an issue, name an intervention, and narrate development in iconoclastic terms. Hiding the dispersed conditions of learning such as material arrangements, affective shifts, institutional norms, uncertainty, contradiction, and the ways in which I am being influenced and transformed. (ref) The three encounters that follow are read in this spirit. Each begins with what prescribed reflection might notice, and then moves toward what diffractive rereading makes newly visible. Specifically, each encounter is first read through established reflective models such as Kolb, Schön, and Gibbs. Secondly, the same encounters are reread diffractively, with tauira, in order to show what those models illuminate and what they leave intact in the narration of professional growth.” (ref)

## Encounter One: The Slowing of the Room

I returned to a lesson that, on paper, looked strong. The aims were clear, the sequence was structured, and the movement from prior knowledge to analysis and application. In my initial post-lesson reflection, I described the lesson as broadly successful. In retrospect, though, I noted a loss of energy when students were asked to shift from conceptual understanding to creative design. Read through a familiar reflective frame, the encounter was easy to understand as a moment of reflective observation in which I noticed a difficulty in the lesson and later identified pacing as the likely cause. (ref) For instance, applying Gibbs’ cycle, the encounter could be narrated neatly as description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, and action plan. (ref) Through Schön, it might be seen as a moment inviting reflection-on-action allowing for a retrospective consideration of what I should adjust for future practice. (ref)
Each of these models is useful because each gives me a disciplined way of returning to practice. (ref) Nevertheless each also provides me with a particular narration of growth. (ref) I, the teacher, observes, diagnoses, learns, and improves. (ref) Here, the encounter discussed appears as one of professional judgement, and growth appears as a better future intervention. (ref) This is the reason why diffraction becomes necessary. (ref) When I reread the encounter diffractively, the slowing of the room no longer belongs to me as a discrete teaching error. The worksheet directed attention toward correctness, the sequence privileged analytic performance, the desks held students in a particular orientation to paper and screen, and my own voice had already leaned toward completion and visible evidence. I was not simply reflecting on the lesson. I was one of its effects. Here, the problem is that I needed to improve pacing. Yet, I identify the assemblage that had produced a mode of participation in which students could comply without entering into a critical relation with the task or one another. Kolb, Schön, and Gibbs help me notice and organise the encounter. (ref) Diffraction, however, shows that what requires my attention is not merely the subsequent learning, but the entangled conditions through which “the problem” itself came to appear. (ref) Professional growth here is therefore not just about adopting a better technique. It is a displacement of the very narrative that casts the teacher as the central site of explanation. (ref)

## Encounter Two: The Homework that Changed Shape

In another lesson, the shift was smaller and less dramatic. I had planned a conventional written homework task, a short paragraph intended to consolidate conceptual understanding. During the lesson, however, I learned that one student regularly created informational content on YouTube, and in that moment I invited a video submission instead. My immediate reflective note recorded this as an instance of differentiation and student-centred planning. Within Gibbs’ cycle, this could easily become a successful reflective entry describing the event, identifying positive feelings, evaluating the change as beneficial, analysing the learner’s needs, and concluding that I should remain flexible in future. (ref) Through Kolb, it could be framed as concrete experience followed by reflective observation and then abstract learning about differentiation. (ref) Through Schön, it might appear as a practitioner responding thoughtfully to the uniqueness of practice. (ref)
Again, these readings tend towards growth and development. (ref) They are professionally credible and even desirable. (ref) Yet they also leave something crucial intact. The teacher remains the subject who adapts, learns, and narrates growth through cultivating responsiveness. (ref) A diffractive rereading disturbs that convenience. (ref) The encounter exposed how strongly I default to written prose as the proper medium of seriousness, rigour, and assessable understanding. The student’s enthusiasm did not confirm a good pedagogical choice. It interrupted the material-discursive preferences through which my own authority is usually secured. What changed was not just the format of the homework, but the arrangement of medium, legitimacy, and assessment. In a conventional reflective model, I become better by learning to offer choice. (ref) In the diffractive reading, I am decentered from the encounter itself. (ref) Growth here begins in the embarrassment of recognising that what I had treated as neutral pedagogy was already saturated with institutional values on what is considered to be actual learning. This encounter therefore becomes a more difficult and more valuable site of professional reflection. It asks not only what I should do next time, but how my teaching has been quietly organised by assumptions that students may interrupt. (ref)

## Encounter Three: The Sadness in the Room

A third encounter remains with me because of its emotional density. In a lesson on schema, stereotype, and culture, a student recognised that a personal view had been shaped by social and cultural conditioning. I noted in the lesson record that the student became confused and sad as this recognition emerged. Within Gibbs’ cycle, I could describe the event, name my concern, evaluate the lesson as pedagogically important but emotionally delicate, and conclude that sensitive issues require greater care and anticipation. (ref) Through Schön, I could regard the moment as one that calls for reflection-in-action, where the practitioner responds to an unfolding situation with tact and uncertainty. (ref) Through Kolb, the event might later become a concrete experience from which I generate a more careful abstract principle for future facilitation. (ref)
These frameworks help make the moment thinkable, especially because the event did call for care, judgement, and responsiveness. (ref) But they also risk translating the encounter too quickly into learning as a reflective professional. (ref) Diffraction resists that closure. (ref) Managing the student’s reaction was not the reason for the sadness in the room. Nor was it only evidence for refining my practice. It was a pedagogical encounter in which concept, identity, memory, cultural history, worksheet, classroom tempo, and institutional expectations became inseparable. My role as a teacher, witness, caretaker, and researcher became unstable.  A reflective cycle can help me process this instability. (ref) A diffractive reading, however, insists that the instability is not an obstacle to reflection but part of what must be reflected with. (ref) The encounter cannot be reduced to the moral that I should handle sensitive topics more carefully, even if that remains partly true. What matters is that learning here happened through discomfort, affect, and relational exposure. Professional growth therefore appears as an altered understanding of pedagogy itself. Teaching critical concepts may also mean inhabiting the affective consequences they set in motion.
